Section 01Thesis and contributions

The claim the paper has to defend, stated so it can be falsified.

Central claim

A network of frozen LLM agents improves measurably across repeated episodes of a long-horizon business simulation when a deterministic harness — not the model — owns memory, validation, and selection. No gradient updates, no fine-tuning, no human editing prompts between episodes. The learning signal lives entirely in plain-text files the system writes for itself.

Contributions to enumerate

  1. A three-network, three-timescale decomposition. An acting network (per turn), a mid-episode analyst (every 10 turns), and a between-episode planner. The networks never share a conversation — they coordinate only through files on disk. Splitting act-from-reflect was measured, not assumed (§6).
  2. Deterministic gates that remove arithmetic and rule-checking from the LLM. FinanceGate (affordability, break-even, research burn) and ProposeAction (~25 syntactic and semantic predicates) run in code. Pre-validation never touches the environment, so a rejected proposal costs a re-prompt and nothing else.
  3. A falsifiable-trial protocol for strategy discovery. Each proposed strategy is written as rule + success condition (metric, direction, day-window) + failure condition, is graded at close-out by a different network against the deterministic log rather than the actor's self-report, and is recorded in an append-only ledger so falsified ideas cannot return.
  4. Champion selection as the compounding mechanism. An episode's plan is promoted to champion only if it beats the best reward on record; every subsequent episode starts from that champion. Pure file copy, no model in the loop. This is the ratchet that makes a bad episode survivable.
  5. A negative result with teeth. The early-abort guardrail — the component that looked most obviously correct — was the single largest drag on performance, for a reason that generalizes to any compounding-reward environment (§9).
  6. An honest accounting of which memory channel actually did the work. Rule-level accretion contributed almost nothing; plan-level selection contributed nearly everything (§10). This reframes the design lesson and is the most publishable surprise in the record.

Section 02The benchmark

Why MAPs, and what makes it a genuine long-horizon credit-assignment problem rather than a puzzle.

Setup to state precisely

  • Mini Amusement Parks (MAPs), Skyfall AI — arXiv:2511.15830. Node backend + a Python map_py interface, exposed to the agents through a vendored MCP server.
  • Configuration used throughout: layout the_islands, difficulty medium, one park, 100 steps per episode, exactly one action per step.
  • Action space: place, move, modify, remove, set_research, survey_guests, wait, over three entity types (ride / shop / staff), each with four subclasses (yellow → blue → green → red).
  • Objective: maximize cumulative park value over the episode.
  • Published difficulty anchor: the MAPs authors report that the best frontier models reach roughly 10% of human performance on medium. That gap is the thing being attacked, and it belongs in the introduction.

Why the task is hard — the four properties to argue

  • Research gating. On medium only yellow subclasses start unlocked. Research costs cash up front ($2k/$8k/$32k per day for slow/medium/fast) and pushes the park into loss before it unlocks anything that pays. Early decisions cap the ceiling of the ending.
  • Compounding. Reward is super-linear in time — the champion episode banked $63,664 by day 50 and $1,861,276 by day 100 (§8). Any evaluation that reads the mid-episode number as a fraction of the final one is wrong by an order of magnitude.
  • Spatial constraint. Attractions need a path-adjacent empty tile; water adjacency adds stacking excitement; some buildable tiles are permanently unreachable by guests ($0 revenue, capacity only). Tile scarcity turns tier upgrades into an eviction problem.
  • Stochasticity and delayed feedback. Breakdowns, guest arrival, and a park rating computed at the start of each day mean an action's effect can surface two days later. Single-day deltas are noise.

Section 03System architecture

What was implemented. The paper's method section — organize it as three networks, two gates, and one loop.

Three networks, three timescales

The player network, per turn

park_director reads park status → strategy_coordinator fans out in parallel to six domain specialists, each of which returns candidate typed actions for its vertical:

Specialists and their separation of concerns
AgentProposesDesign note worth stating

The two deterministic gates

  • FinanceGate — enriches each raw proposal with fields the LLM used to compute by hand (one-time cost from the economics tables, real daily burn read back from the observation, research day-count, break-even days), then approves or rejects against seven ordered rules. Break-even days come from the economics_rides.md table, the same source of truth the specialists read — so the model's judgment and the code's arithmetic never disagree.
  • ProposeAction — validates syntax and legality against the cached observation before the simulator advances at all, then persists the proposal to disk. Checks include: staff must sit on a path or an occupied tile, placements must be path-adjacent, a subclass must actually be researched, unreachable tiles accept only lower-tier evictions, research speeds and topics must be in the legal sets, survey guest counts must be affordable and ≤25.
  • ActionDispatcher — the runner, not an agent, dispatches the approved action exactly once through typed Maps* MCP wrappers. No LLM hop for dispatch.
Method point worth its own paragraph

Rejection is asymmetric and that asymmetry is the design. Pre-validation never touches the environment, so a rejected proposal costs one re-prompt and nothing else — there is no snapshot and no rollback anywhere in the loop. A rejected dispatch is unrecoverable (MAPs always advances the day), so it is logged as a wait and the reason is fed back into the next turn's prompt as literal feedback: text that the coordinator routes to the owning specialist.

Context engineering — the cost-control half of the system

  • Per-specialist status slices. ParkStatus writes seven narrow status_*.json files each turn; each specialist reads only its own. The coordinator never sees the full observation.
  • Phase-only checklist. The episode plan is a day-phased checklist, but ParkStatus surfaces only the line whose turn-range covers the current step. The player never carries the whole plan.
  • TrimHistoryMiddleware — drops everything before the latest human message, so each turn starts from a clean history. This is what makes the player genuinely stateless, and it forces the discipline the whole design leans on: anything worth keeping must be written to disk where another network can read it.
  • StaticPromptCachingMiddleware — caches system message and tool definitions only, deliberately skipping the per-call message-prefix breakpoint that the stock Anthropic caching middleware writes. That breakpoint tags the last message every call, creating a cache entry never reused on the next turn — pure write waste in a loop this shape. Worth reporting as a concrete finding about caching in long agent loops.

Section 04The learning mechanism

The actual research contribution. Frame it as three memory channels with three different lifetimes.

Three memory channels
ChannelLifetimeWritten byWhat it holds
Fixed referencenever changeshumans, onceGame documentation and the per-vertical economics tables. Read-only seed.
Learned rulesdurable, append-onlyplanner at close-outRules confirmed by a trial, each tagged with the episode it came from.
Episode planone episodeplanner at episode startDay-phased checklist + a strategy summary per specialist. Set at the start, never changed mid-episode.

The separation is load-bearing and should be argued explicitly: because the plan and the rules are separate files, a bad episode's plan can be discarded and rolled back to the champion without a single hard-won rule going with it.

The trial protocol

  • The planner proposes at most three candidate strategies per episode — a deliberate cap, added after an earlier version churned through strategies faster than any could prove out.
  • Each is written as a falsifiable hypothesis in three parts: a rule (a general instruction, not a one-off observation), a success condition (metric + direction + a day-window long enough that one lucky day cannot fake it), and a failure condition.
  • A trial steers real decisions for the full episode, then is graded at close-out against the deterministic log, by the planner network — never by the player that made the calls. Verdicts: confirmed → promoted into the learned rules; falsified → dropped and recorded; not_applied → its precondition never fired; inconclusive → carried forward.
  • The ledger (trial_strategies_outcome.md) is append-only and never wiped, so a falsified idea stays dead instead of returning as a "new" proposal three episodes later.
  • A deterministic backstop (CarryoverTrials) retires every trial stamped with an earlier episode at each boundary — no LLM, so nothing can skip it. This exists because a run killed mid-episode otherwise re-arms dead rules with last episode's cash floors.

Champion selection — the ratchet

  • promote_plan(reward, floor) copies plan_current.json → plan_last_good.json only if the episode clears a hard floor and beats the current champion. Pure file operation.
  • Every episode start restores the champion plan as the base, then the planner refines it — so a run always builds on the best strategy, never the most recent one.
  • Playbooks are snapshotted to playbook_history/<ts>_<tag>/ only on promotion. The archive holds best-ever versions, not one directory per boundary. 51 snapshots exist.
  • Three run modes: default (restore champion and improve on it), --resume (continue an in-flight episode untouched), --fresh (move all state to an archive, reset to seeds, strip promoted rules from those seeds — nothing deleted, nothing carried in).

Section 09The abort guardrail

The paper's best negative result. A component that was tested, documented, and obviously sensible — and that was cutting measured performance roughly in half.

What it did

At every mid-episode checkpoint (steps 10, 20 … 90) the watcher emitted VERDICT: on_track | underperforming | doomed, judged against a reward floor. A doomed verdict at or after step 50 aborted the episode immediately; before step 50 it took two consecutive strikes. An aborted episode was fast-forwarded to day 100 with empty wait() calls (the environment has no early reset), its trials were falsified, and the run rolled back to the champion. The rationale was straightforward: stop paying for hopeless episodes.

Crucially, the floor was adaptive — it rose to the best episode's reward as the champion improved (commit 15055c17, 07‑30), with a hard minimum of 350,000.

Why it was wrong

The floor is a terminal quantity being compared against a mid-episode one in a compounding environment. Across all 139 complete episodes on record, the mean ratio of final reward to day-50 reward is 10.0×, and for the strongest episodes it is 25–29×. The consequence is stark: every one of the 139 episodes — 139 of 139 — sat below the 350,000 floor at day 50. The champion itself held just $63,664 at day 50 on its way to $1.86M. The guardrail was therefore firing on essentially every episode from the halfway point onward; which episodes survived came down to whether the analyst happened to return a non-doomed verdict, not to whether the episode was actually failing.

  • 39 of 61 episodes were aborted while the guardrail was active.
  • Mean reward roughly 2.4× higher with the guardrail removed — and still 2.0× higher when compared only against episodes the guardrail let run to completion, so the effect is not merely truncated episodes dragging the mean down.
  • 28 episodes that finished at or above 500k — including the 1,861,276 champion — were below the floor at day 50 and were candidates for the axe.
  • Correlation between day-50 and final reward is r = 0.688: real but far too weak to support a single-strike kill decision, quite apart from the scale error.
Caveat — state this in the paper, do not bury it

This is a natural experiment, not a controlled ablation. The guardrail's removal was not the only change in the working tree at that point: watcher.hocon lost 110 lines, planner.hocon 90, and player.hocon 40, and the runs also began from a stronger champion. The mechanism (§9's floor-scale argument) is independently verifiable from the trajectory data and is strong on its own; the effect size is not yet cleanly attributable. Run the controlled ablation in §12 before claiming a causal 2.4×.

The generalizable lesson

In any environment where reward compounds, an early-termination heuristic must be judged against a trajectory-shaped baseline — the champion's own reward at that same step — not against a terminal target. The system already stored everything needed to do this correctly (per-step logs of every champion episode) and did not use it. That is the fix, and it is a better contribution than simply deleting the guardrail.

Section 10Honest negatives

What did not work as designed. A reviewer will find these; better that the paper gets there first.

The trial mechanism confirmed very little

403 trial outcomes are recorded across the full history. Excluding the 138 carried_over entries (retirement bookkeeping rather than a verdict), 265 trials received a real judgement:

falsified135 · 51%
not_applied97 · 37%
confirmed25 · 9%
inconclusive8 · 3%

Two problems, both worth reporting. A 9% confirmation rate means the planner is a weak strategy generator. Worse, 37% were never applied at all — the hypothesis's precondition never fired inside the episode, so it consumed one of only three trial slots and returned no information. Trial preconditions were written too specifically (often naming exact turn windows and cash floors) for a stochastic 100-day episode to reliably exercise.

Trial coverage was also lopsided by domain: shops 115, rides 89, research 80, staff 68, layout 36, coordinator 14, survey 1 — despite layout and research being where the champion's actual edge came from.

The most important finding in this section

The 1,861,276 champion carries exactly one promoted learned rule — a single research-domain rule tagged (learned ep2). Every other playbook's "Learned rules" section is empty. So the durable rule-accretion channel, which the architecture treats as the primary learning mechanism, contributed almost nothing to the best result. What actually compounded was plan-level selection: the champion checklist, the per-specialist strategy summaries, and the restore-the-best-plan ratchet.

This is a genuine and non-obvious result, and it should be promoted from a limitation to a contribution. The paper's design lesson is not "let agents write down rules"; it is "select whole plans on measured outcome, and let the rules follow."

Other negatives to disclose

  • High variance, small n. Even the best run's episodes span 437,459 to 1,501,444 — a 3.4× spread under an identical configuration. With 12 episodes in the final regime, confidence intervals will be wide. Report them.
  • End-loading needs auditing. The champion earned roughly half its total in the final ten days, and its own README notes a late red-drink swap that took park value from $1.04M to $1.75M in eight steps. If terminal park-value accounting rewards end-loading disproportionately, part of the headline is an accounting artifact rather than better management. This must be checked before publication — it is the first thing a reviewer familiar with MAPs will ask.
  • The ratchet may also be a trap. Always starting from the champion prevents regression but can lock in a local optimum. The convergence on 32 rides / 2 shops, and on "fill unreachable tiles with cheap capacity", suggests the system found one park topology and stopped exploring alternatives.
  • Narrow generalization evidence. One layout (the_islands), one difficulty (medium), one park, one model. No seed control, no replication of a fixed configuration.
  • No in-house baseline. There is no single-agent or no-harness control run in this repository. The leaderboard claim in the earlier write-up is external and undated; either cite it precisely with a retrieval date or run the baseline.
  • Cost is non-trivial. ~$30–40 per episode. A method that needs a dozen episodes to reach its mean is a $400+ proposition per configuration, which bounds how much ablation is affordable — say so, and let it justify the ablation plan's scope.
  • Prior published numbers are stale. The existing write-up's "40× climb, 12,121 → 483,019 over seven episodes" predates every result in §8. Reconcile the two accounts explicitly rather than letting them coexist.

Section 11Threats to validity

Pre-empt these in the paper. Each one is an objection a reviewer will raise.

  • "Your headline ablation is confounded." Correct as things stand — §9's caveat. Fix with the controlled run in §12.
  • "How much of this is the harness and how much is the LLM?" The strongest version of this paper answers it directly: run the same harness driving a scripted greedy or random-legal policy. If the harness alone gets most of the way, the contribution is the harness — which is still a result, but a different one.
  • "You tuned prompts across runs, so this isn't hands-off learning." True within a run, false across the project: prompts changed between runs (see §5). Be precise that within a run nothing is touched, and report the run boundaries honestly.
  • "You're overfitting to one layout." Only a second layout or difficulty answers this.
  • "Contamination." MAPs is public with published documentation; the model may have prior exposure to it or to similar tycoon-genre strategy. Acknowledge and, if possible, test on the sandbox/held-out layouts the benchmark provides.
  • "Not reproducible." Runtime state is gitignored, runs are unseeded, and the final configuration is uncommitted. All three are fixable before submission and all three will otherwise be raised.
  • "Reward hacking." See the end-loading audit in §10.
